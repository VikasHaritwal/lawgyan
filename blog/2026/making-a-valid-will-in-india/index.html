Making a valid will in India: what you actually need

No stamp paper, no compulsory registration, and no lawyer required. But there are a few rules you can't skip.

M

Monika

29 September 2026

Many people put off writing a will because they assume it’s complicated or expensive. In most cases it is neither. What matters is getting a few formalities right, because a will is only tested after you’re gone, when you can no longer explain what you meant.

This article covers wills under the Indian Succession Act, 1925, which applies to most people in India. Muslims are largely governed by their personal law, under which a person generally can’t leave more than one-third of their estate by will without the consent of the heirs.

Who can make a will?

Anyone who is an adult (18 or above) and of sound mind at the time of making it. The will speaks from the date of death, but the mental capacity is judged at the date it was signed.

The three formalities that matter

Section 63 of the Act sets out how a will must be executed:

  1. It must be in writing. Typed or handwritten, in any language. There’s no prescribed format.
  2. You must sign it (or put your mark) in a way that shows you intend it to be your will.
  3. Two or more witnesses must attest it. Each witness must see you sign, and must sign in your presence.

That’s the core of it. A will that meets these three requirements is valid, even on plain paper.

Common myths

“It has to be on stamp paper.” No. There is no stamp duty on a will.

“It has to be registered.” No. Registration is optional. It does, however, make the will harder to challenge and keeps a safe copy with the Sub-Registrar, so it’s often worth doing.

“It has to be made by a lawyer.” No, though a lawyer can help you avoid ambiguity, which is the most common cause of disputes.

Don’t make a beneficiary your witness

This is the single most common mistake. Under Section 67, if a witness (or their spouse) is also a beneficiary, the gift to them is void. The rest of the will survives, but that person loses their share. Choose witnesses who receive nothing under the will.

Other things worth doing

  • Name an executor: a person you trust to carry out the will.
  • Describe property clearly: addresses, survey numbers, account and folio numbers.
  • Say what happens to anything you forgot, with a “residuary” clause.
  • Date every page and sign every page.
  • Keep it safe and tell someone where it is.
  • Review it after major life events: marriage, the birth of a child, a death in the family, or a large purchase.

Can I change it later?

Yes, as often as you like. You can make a fresh will (which should expressly revoke earlier ones) or add a codicil, a short document that amends the will and is executed with the same formalities. The last valid will is the one that counts.

Will my family need probate?

Probate is a court’s certification of a will. It’s compulsory only in certain cases, chiefly wills made by Hindus, Buddhists, Sikhs and Jains within the original areas of Mumbai, Chennai and Kolkata, or dealing with immovable property there. Elsewhere it’s usually optional, though banks and housing societies sometimes ask for it.

The short version: write it down, sign it, have two independent witnesses sign in front of you, and consider registering it. That’s most of what you need.
